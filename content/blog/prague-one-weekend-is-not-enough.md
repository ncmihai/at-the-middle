We have a confession: every time a group of friends asks us where to meet in Central Europe, and we suggest Prague, someone says "but we've already been for a weekend." That is exactly the problem. One weekend in Prague is a trailer, not the film — and after enough trips there ourselves, we decided to write down why we keep going back, and what a long weekend actually costs.

## Vienna's grandeur, without Vienna's prices — and not a knock-off

Prague gets described as "Vienna but cheaper", and we understand why: the baroque facades, the coffee-house culture, the imperial boulevards are all there. But calling it a budget copy does the city a disservice. Prague has its own gravity — slightly rougher, slightly more lived-in, and honestly more fun. A coffee in a Viennese grand café will cost you double what the same coffee costs under the vaulted ceilings of a Prague café on Celetná. Beer halls pour Pilsner Urquell for around €2–3, and the portions in the old-town pubs are the kind that make you plan the next day around a long walk.

What surprised us most on our last trip was the museum density. The National Museum on Wenceslas Square alone is worth a morning — the newly renovated main building is spectacular, and the view from its balcony over the square is one of the best free views in the city. But it is not alone: the Municipal House, the Jewish Museum's synagogues, the Mucha Museum, the KGB Museum, the Strahov Monastery library — you could fill a week and not repeat yourself. We usually tell people to pick two big museums and one weird small one, and leave the rest for the next visit. Which is, of course, the point.

## The metro ride you will remember

Here is something nobody warns you about: the Prague metro has some of the longest escalators in Europe, and Náměstí Míru station has one of the very longest — around 87 metres of escalator dropping you deep underground. The ride takes long enough that you will start wondering if you should have taken the stairs, and then the train arrives in a cavernous, tiled hall that feels like a film set. We rode it twice on our last visit, once out of necessity and once purely for the experience. Take the escalator down at least once. It is the cheapest thrill in the city.

Above ground, the walking does the rest. Prague Castle at the top of the hill, the cobbled lanes of Malá Strana, Charles Bridge at sunrise (go early — by 9 a.m. it is a queue), and the Old Town Square with its astronomical clock. The beer halls — U Fleků, U Zlatého Tygra, Lokál — are where the evenings happen. Our rule: one big hall for dinner, one small local for the last beer of the night.

## The practical part: what a long weekend actually costs

We like to be concrete, so here is what we actually spent per person on a long weekend (Friday to Monday) in Prague, in EUR:

- **Flights**: €60–150 return from most of Western Europe with low-cost carriers; €150–250 from Scandinavia or the UK if you book late. Book 4–6 weeks ahead and you will usually land in the lower half.
- **Stay**: €45–80 per night for a decent apartment or a 3-star hotel in a walkable area (Vinohrady, Žižkov or near the castle side of Malá Strana). Three nights: roughly €135–240.
- **Food and drink**: €25–40 per day. Prague is still one of the cheapest capitals in the EU for eating out — a proper two-course lunch with a beer is €12–18.
- **Transport and entries**: €10–20 for the whole weekend. The metro is cheap, many churches and viewpoints are free, and museum tickets run €8–15 each.
- **Total per person**: realistically **€300–550** for a long weekend, all in, depending mostly on your flights and how fancy your hotel is.

Best time to fly: late spring (May–June) or early autumn (September–October) — dry, mild, and the beer gardens are open. December is magical but crowded and cold; July and August are hot and packed. If your group is flexible, a Thursday-to-Monday window often beats Friday-to-Sunday on price by a surprising margin.

And how many days do you actually need? A weekend covers the Old Town, the castle and one evening in a beer hall. Four days lets you add Malá Strana properly, a day trip to Kutná Hora or Český Krumlov, and a second museum deep-dive. We would not plan less than three full days — the city rewards slow mornings.

## Why Prague is the perfect middle

Here is the part that matters for us: when your friends are scattered across Europe — say Lisbon, Berlin, Stockholm and Athens — Prague is one of those rare cities where everyone can arrive within a day of each other, often on direct low-cost flights, without anyone taking a red-eye or emptying their wallet.
